Between Brussels and Moscow

Authoritarian learning as a tool of power consolidation in Georgia

Brussels Tbilisi Moscow

Abstract

How Georgia’s ruling party borrowed the survival tactics of other authoritarian regimes, and what it cost the country.

01

Introduction

The puzzle

In shortAfter 2022, Georgia’s ruling party kept promising Europe while borrowing the methods of regimes that reject it.

For two decades Georgia pointed west. After the Rose Revolution of 2003, the government of the United National Movement (UNM) rebuilt a weak state, fought corruption, and made membership of the European Union and NATO its main goal.

In 2012 a new party, Georgian Dream (GD), led by Bidzina Ivanishvili, won the elections. It was the country’s first peaceful transfer of power, and at first it looked like a step forward for democracy. Over the next ten years it turned out differently. Checks on the government weakened. Accountability narrowed. Opponents were treated as illegitimate. Researchers describe the period as a gradual erosion of democracy.

Then, in February 2022, Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, and the erosion sped up.

Here is the puzzle. Georgian Dream kept calling itself pro-European. It applied for EU candidate status and received it. At the same time it adopted anti-Western language and tools that looked more and more like those used in Russia, Belarus and Hungary. The clearest example is the “Foreign Agents Law”, which treats non-governmental organisations (NGOs) as instruments of foreign influence.

Research questionHow did authoritarian learning help Georgian Dream consolidate power after 2022?

“Authoritarian learning” means that rulers study what worked and what failed for other rulers, and adjust. The next chapter explains the idea. First, the timeline.

02

Theory

What is authoritarian learning?

In shortRegimes watch what worked and what failed, at home and abroad, and adjust their tactics to stay in power.

Older research treated authoritarian regimes as static. They lasted because they had force and structure on their side, and when they changed, it was read as decay. Newer work, above all by Stephen Hall, sees something else: rulers who are never sure they will survive, and who therefore watch closely.

Authoritarian learning is the name for that watching. Rulers and the elites around them draw lessons from experience, their own and other people’s, and adjust how they rule. The aim is always the same: to stay in power.

Two cautions matter. First, learning is adaptation, not copying. A tool taken from abroad is reshaped to fit local conditions. Second, as Hall and Ambrosio put it, “not all change is learning”. Two regimes can arrive at the same tactic independently.

Why regimes watch so nervously

Hall uses the image of a bank run. A bank can fail even when it has money, simply because people believe that everyone else is about to withdraw. A regime works the same way. It is stable as long as people believe it is strong and able to punish.

Timur Kuran called the hidden part of this “preference falsification”: people publicly support a system that they privately oppose, because they fear the consequences. That makes a regime look more secure than it is. One visible act of defiance can lower the threshold for the next person, and the next. This is the bandwagon effect.

Aside

Learning from other people’s revolutions

Between 2000 and 2005, mass protests removed governments in Serbia, Georgia, Ukraine and Kyrgyzstan. For rulers elsewhere these were not separate events. They were proof that a united opposition, election monitoring and street protest could bring a government down. A Russian commentator, quoted by Hall and Ambrosio, summed up the fear: “The day before yesterday: Belgrade. Yesterday: Tbilisi. Today: Kyiv. Tomorrow: Moscow.”

The response was practical. During Ukraine’s Orange Revolution in 2004, staff from the Belarusian embassy went to the protests in Kyiv, watched how they were organised, and reported back to the security services in Minsk.

The Arab uprisings showed the same pattern in three steps: watch others fall and secure your own elites and forces; shape what people expect and raise the cost of protest; then contain the threat with force and alliances.

Where learning shows

Learning itself happens behind closed doors. What can be observed are its results, in three places.

Rhetorical
What is said: narratives, labels, justifications.
Institutional
What is written into law.
Operational
How police, surveillance and protest control are used.

This thesis looks mainly at the first. Language is what explains and justifies the other two.

03

Methodology

Research design

In shortThe study analyses 386 public statements by two prime ministers, compares them with three reference regimes, and tests the reading with nine experts.

The design is qualitative and interpretive. It is a single case study of Georgia, from February 2022 to the end of 2025. Three methods check each other. Researchers call this triangulation.

386statements analysed
9expert interviews
3reference regimes

Five lenses on a sentence

The Discourse-Historical Approach asks five questions of every statement. The examples come from the second checkpoint, in 2023.

What this method cannot show

Authoritarian learning is hard to observe. It travels through informal channels and closed elite networks. No document says where a tactic came from.

So the findings are inferential. The strongest evidence is not a single law or phrase. It is the timing, the sequence and the shared logic behind decisions. The corpus has limits too: two speakers, one outlet, nothing from social media and nothing said off the record. And a single case does not generalise.

04.2

Analysis

Four checkpoints

In shortAcross four moments, the ruling party’s language moved from “peace” to “foreign agents” to “sovereignty”, and then to open conflict with the EU.

This is the core of the thesis. It follows the public statements of two prime ministers across four moments. The statements are not treated as proof of learning; they show how the logic of the language changed.

At each stop, watch how hard the language has become.

Deep dive

The Foreign Agents Law, the “litmus test”

If learning leaves a trace anywhere, it is in law. Documents are easier to compare than conversations. Several of the interviewed experts called the Foreign Agents Law the litmus test.

The ruling party said the law was a copy of the American Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA). Russia had defended its own law with the same comparison. The thesis sets the three side by side.

How the law works

The law does not need to ban or imprison anyone to have an effect. Its first function is symbolic.

04.3

Analysis

Expert voices

In shortNine experts agreed that the similarities are not a coincidence, and warned against calling them proof.

Between 24 March and 17 April 2026, nine people were interviewed: scholars of authoritarianism, specialists on foreign agents laws, a former diplomat and two opposition politicians. All agreed to be named.

The interviews are not evidence in themselves. They serve to test and correct the reading of the discourse. Two of the nine are political opponents of the ruling party, and their views are reported as views.

Authoritarian learning is a “palette of survival tactics”.

Stephen G. F. Hall, University of Bath. Interview, 31 March 2026

Sovereignty has shifted from the democratic nation to the ruler.

Ghia Nodia, paraphrased. Interview, 30 March 2026

Where they agreed

  • 2022 accelerated a process that had begun earlier. Several date it to 2012.
  • The similarities with Russia, Belarus and Hungary are not a coincidence.
  • The Foreign Agents Law is the clearest trace of learning.

Where they differed

They disagreed on why the pro-European line lasted so long. For Badridze it was imitation that collapsed once the EU asked for real reform. For Bokeria it was a way to take the benefits of Europe without the transformation. For Nodia the war changed the regime’s cost-benefit calculation. For Inauri the pretence was simply no longer needed.

05

Synthesis and conclusion

Key findings

In shortAuthoritarian learning made consolidation cheaper and easier to justify. The price was paid in sovereignty.

  1. 1

    Cheaper experimentation

    Georgian Dream did not invent its tools from scratch. It adapted tools that had already been tested elsewhere. That lowered the risk of failure and made consolidation faster.

    The Foreign Agents Law is the sharpest example: a legal and rhetorical model already in use in Russia, defended with the same arguments, then used against similar actors.

  2. 2

    A playbook of legitimizing language

    Force alone was not enough. The ruling party also needed words that made its decisions sound reasonable. Authoritarian learning supplied them: not only what to do, but how to justify it.

  3. 3

    Authoritarian promotion and the trade-off

    Consolidation at home and geopolitics are one story. The turn against the West did two things at once. Inside Georgia it delegitimized the opposition, civil society and protest. Outside, it weakened the country’s links with Western institutions.

    The ruling party presented this as a defence of sovereignty. In practice it narrowed Georgia’s real sovereign choice, European integration, and expanded the influence of the country that occupies about 20% of Georgia’s territory.

What this means

Georgia’s turn after 2022 was not only one more stage of democratic decline at home. It was also shaped from outside, by a repertoire that Russia promotes and that other governments had already tested. Authoritarian learning gave the ruling party two things: a language that made its decisions look legitimate, and tools that were cheap to try because others had tried them first.

The thesis does not claim to prove direct instructions from Moscow. It shows that the timing, the sequence and the logic of the government’s decisions match repertoires used elsewhere. And it points to a cost that the word “sovereignty” hides: tactics that keep a government in power can leave the country with fewer real choices.

Future research

Two next steps follow. The first is a comparative study built on primary sources from each country, not on secondary literature. It could identify direct links with more confidence. The second is theoretical: authoritarian promotion needs frameworks from international politics, so that the goals of learning can be connected to its geopolitical results.
